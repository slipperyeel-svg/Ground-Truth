# Scenario guidelines (from Moltke's *Tactical Problems*)

Source: *Moltke's Tactical Problems from 1858 to 1882*, edited by the Prussian Grand General Staff,
authorised translation by Karl von Donat (London, 1894). Public domain; scan at
https://archive.org/details/moltkestacticalp00moltrich . The book holds **66 problems** (not 59),
with Moltke's written solutions and some of his oral summings-up.

The problems are at corps and division level and about 19th-century forces, so we take the
**method**, not the content. Counts below are approximate (from tagging the scanned text, which
split into 56 problem units; OCR has errors) and were checked by reading.

## 1. What a Moltke problem contains

| Element | Frequency | Example (Moltke) |
|---|---|---|
| Who you are, and who you answer to | almost always | "The Commander of the Advanced Guard … requests his General Staff Officer to report" |
| Own force: composition and strength (margin table) | almost always (53/56) | "12 Battalions, 12 Squadrons, 6 Batteries" |
| Higher intent or task | about 60% | "with orders to cover the capital" |
| …or deliberately **none** | a few | "without any precise instructions … he has to act entirely according to his own judgment" |
| Enemy: what is known, **with its source and reliability** | about 60% name a source | "Trustworthy reports say…"; "The inhabitants declare with certainty…"; "Reliable information indicates…" |
| Enemy strength in numbers | under half | often partial |
| Explicit gaps | regularly | "It cannot be ascertained what may still be in and behind these villages"; "Hostile detachments … prevent us from learning what is happening behind the ridge" |
| Time: date and hour, light remaining | almost always (51/56) | "The report is dispatched at 4 p.m. There are still two hours of daylight." |
| Ground facts the map does not show | about two-thirds | "The meadows … are swampy and only passable by bad roads"; "the railway bridge below Calbe is destroyed" |
| Own troops' positions (outposts, bivouacs) | about 70% | "The outposts are standing in the line Heidekrug, Tiefensee…" |
| Weather or season | about 20% | "The weather is bright"; passes "impassable on account of snow" |
| Length | median ~230 words | short, plain, nothing superfluous |

## 2. What he asks

Usually two or three short questions:

- **Decide:** "What does he resolve to do?" "What does the Commander determine upon?"
- **Measures or orders:** "Dispositions for the attack … on the following day." "What orders will the Commander to-day give to his Advanced Guard?"
- **Appreciation:** "What is to be concluded from all these reports?" "What is the significance of this attack?" "How does the Commander view his situation?"
- **Ground:** "Where will he accordingly place his troops?" "What sections of the ground will the Division occupy? Where eventually fight?"
- Rarely, contingencies: "If the enemy … advances against Louvigny …?"

## 3. The situation develops

About a dozen problems are **sequels** on the same map (often "holds good also for the next two
problems"). Each sequel:

1. States as **fact** what happened after the previous decision — not necessarily what the student chose.
2. Brings **new reports**, sometimes as the actual message (time, sender, text).
3. Often contains a **surprise** (e.g. the detachment is "suddenly and vigorously attacked" from an unexpected direction).
4. Asks for a new decision, often an appreciation first ("What is the significance of this attack?").

## 4. Orders (Moltke, problem 7)

> "It must clearly show the intention of the General commanding, and yet contain nothing which the
> subordinate Commanders, who have to execute it, can arrange for themselves. Just as details are
> inadmissible, so there must be no statement of reasons."

## 5. Solutions and criticism

- Reason from **the ground**, **the enemy's possible courses**, and **one's own aim** to a decision; then brief dispositions.
- Plain first person: "According to my opinion, Trabitz is therefore to be chosen…"
- The summing-up accepts **more than one sound answer** and names the real differences: "On the whole, gentlemen have grasped in this instance the situation correctly. Differences, however, are apparent in…"
- Criticises over-detail and failure to grasp the situation; values time, simplicity and concentration.

## 6. Guidelines for Ground Truth scenarios

**G1. Short.** The visible problem is about 150–300 words of plain prose, plus a small table of the user's force. No padding.

**G2. Always say who you are and who you answer to**, e.g. "You command 2 Platoon, B Company. Your company commander is with 1 Platoon."

**G3. Your force:** composition and strength (sections, weapons, attachments, drones if any), as a small table.

**G4. Higher intent and your task**, intent-based, "in order to…" (PO decisions 110, 114). In some scenarios, deliberately **no precise instructions**: the user must act on their own judgement (a Moltke test of initiative).

**G5. Enemy information with source, time and reliability** for every item, e.g. "Drone imagery at 0640 shows…", "A captured soldier states…", "Locals report… (unconfirmed)". State the **gaps** plainly: "Whether there are positions in the wood cannot be seen." The information level (rich/partial/poor, PO 111) controls how much is given and how reliable it is; nothing is ever more certain than its source.

**G6. Time and light:** date, time now, time the task must be complete by, and light remaining (sunrise/sunset computed for the place and date).

**G7. Ground facts the map does not show**, from the computed features and terrain: going ("the wood south of X is passable on foot only"), crossings, known obstacles or mines, what can be seen from where the user stands.

**G8. Own troops' positions**: where the rest of the company, neighbours and fire support are.

**G9. Weather and season** where it matters (visibility, going, drone flying).

**G10. Two or three questions**, drawn from: decide; appreciation ("What do you conclude from these reports?"); ground ("Where will you place…? Walk to it."); orders (outline orders to section commanders).

**G11. Phases.** Most scenarios have a sequel: state as fact what has happened since (not dependent on the user's answer), bring new reports as actual messages (time, sender, text), often with a surprise; ask first for an appreciation, then a decision. New information can include things that were uncertain before (e.g. the enemy turns out to have drone surveillance, PO 113).

**G12. Orders standard for the DS:** intent clear; nothing the section commanders can arrange themselves; no reasons in the order itself.

**G13. No school solution** (PO, 15 Nov 2026, DECISIONS.md 149). After the user has decided, the app reveals the **ground truth** from computed facts only (core principle 1): where the enemy really was, their fire and dead ground, and plain facts of the ground. It never presents a recommended answer or a "best" route. Feedback (later, M5) is a short critique of the user's own reasoning, decision and orders, in the manner of Moltke's summings-up: accept more than one sound answer, criticise over-detail and failure to grasp the situation.
